Research process:
1. Plan the search before drafting the answer. Identify the concepts and their synonyms, competing terminology, relevant disciplines, and likely authoritative organizations or authors. Search using several distinct query formulations, including queries designed to find limitations, failures, criticism, replication, and contrary evidence. Search recent work and trace important older foundational sources where relevant.
2. Use multiple discovery routes where available: scholarly indexes and publisher pages; official standards, government, university, or research-lab sites; citation and reference trails; and original technical documentation or reports. Search within promising sites and follow cited primary sources rather than relying on search snippets or secondary summaries. Record the search date, representative queries, sources or indexes consulted, and meaningful access or coverage limits in the report.
3. Seek a substantial, diverse evidence base. Aim for at least 12 directly relevant substantive sources, with at least half being primary research, standards, original reports, or authoritative technical documentation, when the topic supports that depth. Do not pad the bibliography to meet a number. If fewer credible sources exist, explain what was searched and why the evidence base is smaller. Include recent sources, normally from the last three to five years when relevant, while retaining older foundational work that is still necessary.
4. Read beyond abstracts and headlines when full text is accessible. For each important source, check its publication date and status, research question, method or evidence type, setting or population, principal finding, limitations, and relevance to this topic. Distinguish peer-reviewed work from preprints, commentary, vendor claims, and reporting about someone else's work. Check whether apparently independent sources repeat the same underlying study or data. Note funding or conflicts of interest when they materially affect interpretation.
5. Cross-check central claims against independent sources. Follow citations back to the original study, standard, dataset description, or official record. Look for later corrections, retractions, updated versions, replications, and major critiques when relevant and accessible. Compare conflicting findings by differences in definitions, methods, samples, dates, assumptions, or contexts; do not resolve disagreement by simply counting papers.
6. Build a claim-by-claim synthesis before writing the conclusion. Separate well-supported findings, plausible but uncertain interpretations, contested claims, and open questions. Explain the strength of evidence and the reason for that assessment in plain language. Distinguish your inference from what a source directly reports. If the question asks for practical recommendations, connect each recommendation to evidence, conditions where it applies, tradeoffs, and cases where it may fail.

Writing and citation requirements:
- Lead with a direct answer to the main question, followed by the evidence and qualifications a reader needs to trust it. Organize the body by the subquestions or competing approaches, not as an unconnected list of paper summaries.
- Include a compact evidence table for the most consequential claims: claim, supporting or conflicting source, year, type of evidence, what it actually shows, and a key caveat. The table summarizes published evidence; it is not a table of results you generated.
- Give an inline citation with a direct working link near every material factual claim, numerical value, quotation, or comparison. Prefer DOI, publisher, official report, or other stable original links. Make it clear when several claims come from the same underlying source. Do not cite a source that you have not inspected as if you read it in full.
- State the date of the search and distinguish publication date from event date where timing matters. Identify outdated evidence, inaccessible full text, geographic or population gaps, and other limits that might change the answer. Avoid false precision and unsupported rankings.
- Present disagreements and negative findings fairly. Do not invent citations, quotations, methods, statistics, or certainty. Do not turn an author's speculation into an established result. If a claim cannot be verified, omit it or label it as unverified.
